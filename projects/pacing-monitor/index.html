PRODUCT STORY 02 · MOBILEACTION

From one customer's requirement to a product for many.

How I helped turn an enterprise advertiser's campaign-monitoring needs into a configurable feature for MobileAction's broader customer base.

Product
Pacing Monitor
Company
MobileAction
Role
Product Manager
Domain
Enterprise SaaS · Apple Ads campaign management
Journey
Customer discovery → Product design → Delivery → Release

Released to all eligible customers and adopted by the original enterprise advertiser.

01 · The starting point

A migration requirement revealed a broader product opportunity.

An enterprise advertiser preparing to move to MobileAction already relied on a pacing-monitoring workflow. They needed to know whether their advertising campaigns were progressing toward business targets, rather than only looking backward at reported performance.

Maintaining that capability mattered to their transition. But building a one-off copy of another system would have missed a bigger opportunity: a reusable way for advertisers to define what on track meant for their own campaigns.

My role was to translate that customer need into a practical, configurable product that could fit MobileAction's existing campaign-management experience.

02 · Discovery and framing

The question wasn't just “What does the customer want copied?” It was “What decision do they need to make?”

Through direct customer conversations and Customer Success feedback, we explored the monitoring decisions behind the request: which campaigns mattered, which performance indicators defined success, how targets should change over a monitoring period, and what should count as a risk.

This shifted the product framing from a customer-specific migration feature toward a reusable monitoring capability. It also shaped the MVP: enough flexibility to accommodate different advertiser objectives without creating a separate analytics product.

Solve for the decision behind the request, not just the workflow being requested.

03 · Shaping the MVP

From complex configuration to a manageable sequence.

I created initial wireframes and worked with a product designer to structure the creation experience around four decisions:

  1. Set the monitoring period: Establish the time window for comparing expected and actual performance.
  2. Choose campaigns: Select the advertising activity to monitor.
  3. Define KPIs and targets: Specify the performance measures and business goals that matter.
  4. Set risk boundaries: Configure the ranges that indicate when performance may need attention.

The monitoring experience brought current and expected values, pacing information, and risk classifications into a focused dashboard. Where users needed more detailed investigation, the feature linked back into MobileAction's existing Ads Manager rather than duplicating reporting capabilities.

04 · Where product decisions became technical decisions

A pacing dashboard is only trustworthy if the business logic behind it is right.

Good product design sometimes means defining what a feature should not try to become.

05 · From specifications to delivery

Making detailed business rules buildable and testable.

I prepared a detailed PRD covering the creation, editing, viewing, and overview flows, including KPI behavior, targets, formulas, risk thresholds, attribution dependencies, validation, error handling, and navigation. I managed the backlog and delivery planning, collaborated with Engineering on feasibility and implementation questions, and clarified acceptance criteria where required.

I also checked implemented journeys against the product requirements and reviewed issues with QA. QA owned formal testing; my focus was ensuring that the business logic and experience we had agreed on were reflected in the released product.

Customer validation happened through direct sessions and Customer Success feedback, with particular attention to the advertiser whose migration need had started the work.

06 · Release and outcome

From a customer need to a released product capability.

Pacing Monitor was released to all eligible MobileAction customers. The original migrating enterprise advertiser adopted and used the feature.

That is the verified outcome: the customer retained an important monitoring workflow, and MobileAction gained a reusable product capability for a wider customer base.

No revenue impact, retention improvement, adoption rate, time saved, or specific commercial conversion is claimed for Pacing Monitor because those figures have not been established.

07 · Looking back

The lesson: identify the reusable product within the specific request.

This project reinforced the importance of investigating why an enterprise customer needs a feature before choosing what to build. It also deepened my appreciation for the logic beneath analytics interfaces: target calculations, attribution behavior, and risk classification can matter as much as the visual design.

The result was not simply a replacement for one customer's previous workflow. It was an opportunity to turn a real customer constraint into a focused, configurable feature that fit the existing platform.

A customer request is a starting point. Product thinking turns it into something useful beyond the original conversation.
Additional documented product detail

MVP scope, metric calculations, and configurable risk rules

A reusable monitoring model

The MVP covered dashboard creation and editing, a detailed dashboard view, and an overview of multiple dashboard cards. A dashboard could monitor one app and campaign-group context, contain up to five named groups of campaigns, and track up to five selected metrics. These limits reflected deliberate MVP scoping and UX simplification rather than a claim that customers would never need more flexibility. Users selected a starting date and business-cycle duration, chose campaigns from the platform’s existing campaign selector, selected relevant performance metrics, and assigned target values per campaign group. Pacing Monitor then evaluated actual results against expected performance and the user's chosen risk boundaries.

Expected progress and suggested daily targets

As PM, I defined the expected behavior for pacing calculations and collaborated with Engineering to validate feasibility. The PRD distinguished cumulative measures such as spend, impressions and installs from ratio measures such as TTR and conversion rate. For cumulative metrics, Expected Value = (Days Elapsed × Target Value) ÷ Total Cycle Days. For ratio metrics, Expected Value = Target Value. For applicable cumulative metrics, Suggested Daily Target = (Target − Current Value) ÷ Days Remaining. These formulas represented business-level expected behavior; Engineering owned the implementation. For example, if a campaign group had a target of 10,000 installs, had delivered 6,000 and had ten days remaining, the suggested daily target would be 400 installs. This is an illustrative calculation, not a customer outcome.

Configurable risk monitoring

Risk states were calculated relative to the expected target, using 100% as the reference point. Users could configure moderate- and high-risk thresholds both below and above the target. The PRD provided initial defaults, including below-target moderate risk at under 80% and high risk under 65%, and above-target moderate risk above 120% and high risk above 145%. Threshold controls included synchronized slider handles and numerical inputs, letting users tune risk classifications to the metric and business objective. This mattered because falling short of an install goal and exceeding a spend goal can represent different problems.

Interaction and validation behavior

I prepared initial wireframes for the four-stage creation flow and collaborated with a product designer to refine the interface. We aimed to make a technically detailed configuration process understandable by presenting the decisions in a logical order: define the monitoring period, choose the campaigns, specify KPIs and targets, then define risk boundaries. The dashboard view brought the same model into a compact monitoring experience. It showed current and expected values, the ratio between them, suggested daily targets where applicable, and risk classification. The overview surfaced cards for different dashboards, making it easier to see which monitoring areas needed attention. I also specified validation and interaction behavior: required-field guidance, unavailable steps until prerequisites were completed, creation success and failure notifications, unsaved-change confirmation, and appropriate scheduled states for dashboards with future start dates.

Previously documented scope and platform trade-offs

Decision A: Configurability versus usability

We limited each dashboard to one app and campaign-group context, up to five monitoring groups and five metrics. The intent was to simplify first-time setup without removing the ability to create multiple dashboards for distinct monitoring needs.

Decision B: Standard risk rules versus customer-defined goals

A universal threshold model would have been easier to configure but less representative of different advertisers' priorities. We kept configurable above- and below-target thresholds so users could decide what constituted moderate and high risk for each metric.

Decision C: New monitoring versus duplicating Ads Manager

Pacing Monitor was not intended to replicate every campaign reporting view. Instead, users could open relevant campaigns in Ads Manager with filters pre-applied. This linked risk identification to deeper investigation while reusing existing reporting capabilities.

Decision D: Independent attribution versus platform consistency

Rather than invent separate attribution rules for Pacing Monitor, we reused established platform settings and documented their dependencies. The PRD described when attribution options should appear, how conflicting metric types should be handled and what warnings users should see when changing attribution mode.

Calculation, attribution, and reporting dependencies

Different calculation semantics

Pacing formulas had to respect whether a metric accumulated over time or represented a rate or average. Treating all metrics as cumulative would have produced misleading expected values. I documented the distinctions and confirmed feasibility with Engineering.

Attribution-dependent metrics

Some conversion and cohort metrics depended on attribution settings such as install time versus event time. The PRD defined conditions for displaying settings and warning users when a change affected selected metric compatibility. The solution reused the platform's existing attribution behavior to preserve consistency.

Monitoring-to-reporting navigation

The feature needed to preserve context when navigating back to detailed reporting. I specified links to Ads Manager with selected campaign filters already applied, allowing users to investigate an issue without recreating their campaign selection manually.

Retrospective product hypothesis and positioning

The migration created an immediate need for feature continuity, but the principal strategic opportunity was broader differentiation. Pacing Monitor could extend MobileAction beyond retrospective reporting toward proactive evaluation of whether performance was on pace to meet goals. Product hypothesis (retrospectively formulated): If advertisers could group campaigns, define targets over business cycles and see meaningful risk signals, they could identify deviations earlier and make more timely optimization decisions with less manual analysis. The feature was positioned inside the existing SearchAds environment. The PRD specified a gated introduction for free accounts with a Contact Sales action; however, this case study makes no claim about pricing, conversion or revenue generated by that positioning.

Product context and illustrative calculation

MobileAction’s SearchAds product allows advertisers to manage and optimize Apple Ads campaigns. Its reporting tools display metrics such as spend, impressions, taps, installs and acquisition costs. But current performance alone does not answer a forward-looking question: is a set of campaigns progressing fast enough to reach a business target before the monitoring period ends? Consider an advertiser aiming for 10,000 installs in a defined period. Knowing that 6,000 installs have been delivered is informative, but assessing whether performance is on track requires knowing how much of the cycle has elapsed, how the target is distributed over time and what variation from expected progress the business accepts. Pacing monitoring brings these elements together.
