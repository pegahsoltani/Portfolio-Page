CPP A/B Testing

Turning creative guesswork into confident decisions

How I helped take an Apple Ads experimentation feature from customer discovery to beta and public release at MobileAction.

MobileAction

CPP A/B Testing · B2B SaaS · Apple Ads · Product Management · 2025

The result: Two enterprise advertisers completed tests and applied what they learned. In follow-on campaign performance, they observed approximately 15–20% improvements in tap-through rate (TTR). The feature progressed to public availability in MobileAction’s enterprise offering.

Public product illustration from MobileAction material. The screenshots throughout this case study show the released product, not my original internal wireframes or necessarily the first beta UI.

What we built — experimentation inside Apple Ads campaign management

CPP A/B Testing is a feature of MobileAction’s Apple Ads Campaign Management Platform. It helps mobile app marketers compare Custom Product Pages — alternative App Store product pages with different screenshots and creative messages — and understand which one performs better for a particular advertising setup. Instead of manually creating, rotating and monitoring every test variation, advertisers choose what to compare; the platform handles the test entities, timing and performance monitoring, then shows the results alongside confidence signals.

It sounds straightforward. In reality, designing it meant finding a safe balance between automation, experimental isolation, statistical reliability and everyday campaign operations.

My role — connecting advertiser needs to a usable experiment

Product Manager, MobileAction | Approximately six months, beginning April 2025

I owned the product-management work across further discovery, requirements, initial wireframes, backlog and delivery coordination, acceptance validation, customer beta feedback, and release enablement. I worked directly with advertisers and partnered with Customer Success, Product Design, Backend, Frontend, QA, Data Science, Sales, and Marketing.

This was a cross-functional product. Data Science owned the statistical methodology, Engineering built the integrations and execution logic, and Design helped refine the interface. My responsibility was to bring those disciplines together around the right customer problem, make the trade-offs explicit, and get an understandable, usable solution into customers’ hands.

The moment that made the problem tangible

Imagine preparing for a high-stakes shopping season with several custom App Store pages: one emphasizing a promotion, another emphasizing the product range, and another speaking to a different audience. Which page should appear in an Apple Ads campaign?

That was a real decision facing the gaming and retail/e-commerce advertisers I spoke with. They had multiple Custom Product Pages (CPPs) for different audiences and storefronts, yet selecting the most promising creative often relied on intuition or manually assembled comparisons. Before seasonal peaks such as Black Friday, the cost of choosing poorly felt especially consequential.

One practical recommendation I made was to run an experiment before the peak season, so a customer could enter it with evidence about which variant to use rather than switching creatives on instinct.

But the underlying problem went beyond finding time to run a test.

Discovery — the test itself was not always trustworthy

The demand for CPP testing already existed when I joined. Customer Success and Sales had heard the need, and my job was to understand how advertisers were actually trying to answer their questions. I worked with Customer Success to identify relevant accounts and held conversations with advertisers about their existing workflows.

Two problems kept appearing together: setting up and monitoring experiments manually took effort, and the experiment environment was often not isolated enough to support a clean conclusion.

Advertisers described three different patterns:

Parallel comparisons: Run multiple creatives at once, often using similar campaigns or ad groups. Apple Ads distributed traffic between them, but the resulting exposure could be uneven; a seemingly stronger result might reflect differences in traffic, not just the product page.

Switch comparisons: Show one CPP for a period and then switch to another. This reduced concurrent overlap but made the comparison vulnerable to changes in weekday, time of day, seasonal demand, or campaign conditions.

Reverse comparisons: Start with a CPP and ask which ad group, audience, keyword setup, or campaign configuration performed best with it — not only which CPP was best for an existing ad group.

We needed to answer three questions before a test result could be actionable: Were we comparing like with like? Did we have enough data? And could the advertiser understand the result?

That reframed my product goal. A one-click version of an unreliable manual test would not have been a good product. I wanted a workflow that made experimentation easier and made the conclusion more defensible.

The opportunity — make a complex experiment feel approachable

My working hypothesis was that advertisers would make better creative decisions if a single product could set up comparable experiments, manage test traffic and timing, calculate a useful duration, and present performance and confidence signals without requiring every user to become a statistician.

For MobileAction, this also represented a differentiation opportunity. In our competitive research at the time, we had not identified an equivalent integrated capability among the Apple Ads management tools we reviewed. We chose to position the feature in the enterprise offering, rather than introduce a separate paid product.

The released test dashboard provided a dedicated entry point and a view of experiment status. Screenshot from MobileAction’s public Help Center.

Shaping the MVP around real customer decisions

Instead of forcing every advertiser into one generic A/B-test template, we defined four configurations across two testing methods. All four belonged to the initial product scope because they reflected distinct questions uncovered during discovery.

01 — Parallel: Which CPP works best for this ad group?

One ad group, up to four product-page variants, shown during the same period. This was the direct creative-comparison workflow: keep the underlying setup comparable, then inspect how the pages perform. It was especially useful when simultaneous exposure would reduce the effect of changing market conditions.

Single-ad-group Parallel setup. The original ad group is paused and test entities are used for the experiment.

02 — Parallel: Which ad group works best with this CPP?

The reverse question mattered too. Instead of changing the creative, advertisers could hold a single CPP constant and compare up to four ad groups. That opened the door to testing which targeting and campaign configuration paired best with the page.

Selecting multiple ad groups for one product page, supporting the reverse-testing use case.

03 — Switch: How can we compare variants over time?

For advertisers who preferred sequential exposure, the Switch method rotated one active variant at a time. It supported two implementations: Ad Switch, which rotates ads inside one ad group, and Ad Group Switch, which creates separate duplicated ad groups and rotates their active status.

The Switch setup illustrates a meaningful product choice: simpler continuity within an ad group versus stronger isolation using duplicated ad groups.

Ad Group Switch: a more isolated environment, with one variant’s ad group active at a time.

04 — The workflow ties it together

The user journey was intentionally consistent across methods: select the ad group or groups → select the product pages → choose the testing method → review precision and duration → confirm → monitor and interpret.

Step 1: select the existing campaign/ad-group context.

Step 2: choose the creative variants to compare.

Step 3: choose the execution approach before configuring the test.

The difficult decisions happened behind the simple UI

A polished creation form would not be enough. The hardest product work was deciding what must happen when someone pressed Start — and what the interface should prevent them from doing.

Trade-off 1 — User-controlled deadlines vs. reliable duration

Customers naturally wanted a test to fit their campaign calendar, particularly before seasonal peaks. Yet traffic volume and expected conversion behavior determined how much evidence a meaningful comparison required. Letting every advertiser choose any end date could make short tests look authoritative even when they were not.

I brought this tension to Data Science, who defined the statistical methodology. We used established statistical approaches — not new formulas invented by the product team — to connect historical traffic, precision and confidence to estimated test duration. The released workflow uses the selected ad group’s 28 days of historical data and supports a desired margin of error, with 90% as the standard confidence setting.

For the initial release, we prioritized calculated duration over unrestricted manual duration entry. We explored more flexible approaches for future iterations, but the MVP needed to protect the meaning of its results.

The duration is an output of test conditions and statistical settings, not simply an arbitrary calendar preference.

Trade-off 2 — Running a test vs. protecting the test

Real advertising accounts are busy. Bid changes, budget allocation, automated bidding and other campaign actions can continue operating in the background. Those changes may be perfectly appropriate for an active campaign, but can undermine an experiment if they affect variants differently.

We introduced automated setup and controls so the system could duplicate test entities, pause originals where appropriate, manage variant status, and restore originals after a test ended or was stopped. Parallel setups isolated testing from certain automation strategies; Ad Switch could offer a choice about preserving or disabling strategies, acknowledging that different advertisers had different operational needs.

A related complication was traffic allocation. Because Apple Ads determined which simultaneously running variant received traffic, exposure could drift substantially. The Parallel workflow included Stabilize Traffic, which monitors imbalance and can temporarily pause an overexposed variant to let others catch up. It is an option users can disable if uninterrupted delivery matters more for their situation.

Traffic stabilization is a good example of an invisible infrastructure issue becoming a visible, understandable product setting.

Trade-off 3 — Explain the essentials, don’t build a second reporting product

The test results view had to help advertisers interpret core metrics such as impressions, TTR, conversion rate, and confidence signals. Some users also wanted the entire breadth of campaign analytics inside the feature.

Rather than rebuild the full reporting surface, we kept the experimentation view focused and used the existing Ads Manager for deeper analysis of test-generated entities. It was a deliberate MVP boundary: answer the experiment question first; reuse the broader platform for exploration.

From PRD to a product that could safely run

I converted discovery into a PRD with the four testing configurations, user journeys, functional requirements, acceptance criteria, edge cases, and initial wireframes. Together with Design and Engineering, I refined the interface and the technical behavior; with Backend, Frontend, and QA, I prioritized and clarified the implementation backlog.

Two platform constraints became especially important during delivery:

Overnight data availability. Apple Ads performance data did not appear instantly in our platform. An experiment could be successfully configured yet initially display no useful performance results. We addressed the mismatch with a scheduled-for-midnight state and clearer timing around the first meaningful data update.

API status latency. Activating and pausing Apple Ads entities was not instantaneous. For Switch experiments especially, the team had to account for propagation delays and buffer periods so the execution schedule would not assume a status change had completed immediately.

I validated implemented flows against the requirements alongside QA’s formal testing. We prioritized problems that could affect experiment execution, statistical correctness, or completion over attractive but secondary enhancements.

The launch confirmation summarizes the chosen setup before the system creates and manages the test entities.

Beta was where the assumptions met real traffic

We released the feature first to a limited group of customers. I followed their experience through direct conversations and Customer Success feedback, tracked issues in a shared feedback space, and used what we learned to reprioritize the backlog.

Four beta learnings materially improved the product:

1. Low traffic could make a mathematically valid plan commercially impractical. Some campaigns produced estimated tests so long that the result would arrive after the decision was needed. We worked with Data Science on minimum traffic eligibility requirements to avoid encouraging tests unlikely to yield usable answers in a reasonable window.

2. Confidence was easy to misunderstand. A statistical signal could be correct and still leave customers unsure what to do. We refined explanatory language and Help Center guidance so people could interpret confidence and understand when a result was inconclusive.

3. Early confidence could look more decisive than it was. Confidence values sometimes fluctuated dramatically while data was sparse. We delayed displaying those signals until they were sufficiently stable, reducing the risk of premature decisions.

4. Advertisers needed a safe exit. A planned experiment was not always the right experiment a week later. We introduced a Stop action, including a return to the original campaign setup, so customers could move on without manually untangling test entities.

The beta confirmed a principle I now carry into other product work: scientifically valid logic still needs practical guardrails and an understandable interface.

Public release — shipping the feature was only half of adoption

After addressing the most consequential beta findings, we expanded CPP A/B Testing to general availability for eligible enterprise customers. I collaborated with Customer Success, Sales, and Marketing on demos, feature positioning, training, and launch readiness. I also worked on public-facing Help Center education explaining the different setup methods, duration choices, monitoring, and result interpretation.

We looked beyond how many people clicked Create Test. The adoption signals I cared about included test creation, completed tests, repeated or meaningful use, customers acting on a winner, and the number and nature of support issues. A completed experiment that changed a real campaign decision mattered more than an impressive number of abandoned setups.

Public-facing product material helped explain a sophisticated feature to users and internal customer-facing teams.

The outcome — experiments that changed decisions

The clearest proof of value came when customers finished the test and used the findings.

Two enterprise advertisers — including examples in gaming and retail/e-commerce — completed experiments and applied their learnings in subsequent campaign choices. Their later campaign performance showed roughly 15–20% improvements in tap-through rate (TTR) after adopting test-informed changes.

That is a post-test customer outcome, not a claim that the product mechanically lifted every campaign by 15–20%, nor a controlled causal estimate of feature impact. Not every experiment produced a clear winner, and that was also useful: sometimes the honest answer was that the evidence did not justify preferring one creative.

The feature also strengthened MobileAction’s enterprise story. It attracted interest in proof-of-concept and commercial conversations, contributed to an enterprise customer win, and became part of the publicly available enterprise product offering. Customer identities and private campaign data are intentionally omitted here.

What I would do differently

If I were starting again, I would investigate traffic eligibility and realistic test duration even earlier. During discovery, I would use historical traffic to simulate sample experiments with a few representative advertisers — including low-volume accounts — before finalizing the setup experience.

That would help surface a crucial question sooner: Can this customer realistically finish the experiment before they need to make the decision?

I would also explicitly test customers’ comprehension of confidence and inconclusive outcomes earlier, alongside the UI. The experience taught me that the PM’s job is not simply to deliver sophisticated statistical features. It is to translate rigor into a decision a customer can safely make.

The biggest lesson: Better experiments are not the ones with the most settings. They are the ones that help customers trust the comparison, understand its limitations, and actually act on what they learn.
