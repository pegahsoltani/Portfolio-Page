KiddieDesk

Preschool Operations, Reimagined

VenusSoft | Product Manager and Project Consultant | MVP in school use

Company Overview

VenusSoft works with businesses to turn operational problems into software products. One of those projects was KiddieDesk, a multi-tenant SaaS platform designed for nurseries and preschools. The product brings school administration, classroom care records, parent communication, secure child handover, and financial workflows into a shared system.

On paper, those might sound like separate features. In practice, they all revolve around the same people and information: a child, their family, the school, the class, and the staff members authorized to work with those records. The value of the platform depends on those connections remaining accurate from one workflow to the next.

Project Team

I worked on KiddieDesk as a Product Manager and Project Consultant through VenusSoft, collaborating with the customer and with UI/UX and Engineering contributors. My responsibilities covered shaping the product requirements, mapping role-based journeys, defining scope and acceptance criteria, creating functional AI-assisted prototypes, validating the proposed experience with the customer, and coordinating delivery.

I used prototypes to make conversations more concrete. Instead of asking stakeholders to imagine how a requirement might work, I could walk through a proposed journey and discuss exactly what needed to happen on each screen.

Problem Space

The problem wasn't simply that a preschool needed more screens or digital forms. The harder challenge was making everyday work consistent across people with very different responsibilities.

A school director needs to organize classes, manage records, and see where information is missing. A teacher needs to record attendance and care activities while supervising children. A parent wants to understand what happened during their child's day without navigating unrelated administrative material. Meanwhile, sensitive information must only be visible to the people who should have access to it.

A disconnected approach to these tasks creates room for duplicated work, missing context, and inconsistent records. Our product challenge was to connect them without making routine actions more complicated.

That led to a question guiding the design:

How might we make a preschool's daily operations and parent communication feel like one reliable experience—while preserving the correct child, classroom, and access context at every step?

The source documentation establishes these workflow and consistency challenges. It does not provide a quantified pre-product baseline for staff hours saved, administrative errors, or parent satisfaction.

Project Scope

The broader product specification covers school records and enrollment, classroom care, parent communication, secure handover, financial operations, and platform administration. It also defines distinct experiences for school staff and families, with Turkish and English language support.

Rather than treat every module as equally valuable for the first learning cycle, I framed the core experience around a connected daily journey: the school establishes the child and class relationship; the teacher records what happened; the parent sees the right child's information; and management can identify gaps.

The core workflow

  1. School administration: Establish the school, class, child, and family relationships.
  2. Teacher: Select a class, record attendance and care activities, handle exceptions, and confirm the result.
  3. Parent: Select the appropriate child and review the day's information.
  4. School management: Identify incomplete records and follow up.

The portfolio MVP prioritization document identifies this as a recommended pilot value slice, not evidence that all the broader features were excluded from the delivered product. Financial workflows and external-provider integrations were identified as more dependency-heavy areas for a later pilot learning sequence.

Execution

Turning a broad product idea into decisions the team could act on

A product like KiddieDesk has a deceptively large number of edge cases. A teacher might work across more than one class. A parent might have more than one child. A student may be absent, which is different from having no care entry recorded. An announcement being opened is not necessarily the same as a parent deliberately acknowledging it or granting permission.

I structured the work through user journeys, product specifications, acceptance criteria, and cross-role workflows. The goal was to identify not only what users could click, but what the system should do with the information afterward.

I then used AI-assisted, specification-driven prototyping with ChatGPT and OpenAI Codex to bring proposed workflows to life. That let me review the experience more directly before transitioning agreed decisions into design and engineering delivery.

The first important decision: Build around the child's day

One documented UX decision concerned the parent's home screen. Payment information had been given greater prominence, but the product direction shifted to put the child's daily summary first.

That seemingly small change says a lot about the product's priorities. When a parent opens the app, the most immediate question may be, “How was my child's day?” The product should help answer that question before introducing secondary administrative tasks.

The documented implementation work also considered selecting a child, retaining that context during navigation, and presenting the experience on a narrow mobile screen. There is no measured retention or satisfaction uplift attributable to this decision in the available sources.

The second decision: A successful API isn't a finished experience

During delivery, a particularly useful lesson came from comparing the software's underlying capabilities with what people could actually accomplish in the interface.

The project records documented endpoint-to-screen gaps, including a historical check that found 59 endpoints without corresponding UI coverage across seven modules. That was not a current production defect count. It was a dated signal that technical delivery and user-facing delivery could diverge.

The response was to treat each capability as an end-to-end experience. A feature needed a visible entry point, meaningful data, correct role access, and understandable behavior for loading, empty, failure, and permission states—not just an implemented backend route.

That distinction changed the way I evaluated readiness: Can the intended user complete the job, with the correct data and safeguards?

The third decision: Make daily recording efficient without hiding exceptions

For a teacher, repeating the same action child by child can make a routine task unnecessarily lengthy. The documented daily-care journey therefore supports selecting a class, applying a shared entry, changing individual exceptions, reviewing the result, and confirming it.

That design introduces another requirement: one bulk action must not erase important differences between children. The product needs to distinguish children who were absent, entries that were left incomplete, and records that were successfully saved. Multi-class scenarios also need to preserve class-specific outcomes.

I treated these situations as part of the product experience rather than edge cases to leave entirely to engineering interpretation.

Shipping the Iterations

Once the journeys were clearer, the next challenge was maintaining consistency between requirements, screens, and actual system behavior as the product evolved.

A functional prototype is helpful because it exposes unclear decisions quickly. It can also create false confidence: a screen may look complete even when its data, permission rules, or error states have not been implemented. During KiddieDesk's development, additional requests such as classroom-stream, assignment, and notification capabilities were documented as separate scope changes rather than silently treated as part of the original specification.

I used this distinction to keep the product discussion grounded: what had been defined, what had been prototyped, what had been verified in development, and what still required release or customer acceptance.

The development evidence includes dated checks of role isolation, parent daily-summary behavior, multi-class daily work, and browser/API interactions. It also records unresolved native-device, external-provider, file-handling, and production-readiness dependencies. Those checks demonstrate concrete implementation work, but they should not be presented as proof that every specified capability was production-ready.

Results

The meaningful product milestone is that KiddieDesk reached an MVP in use at a school, with rollout to further schools planned, based on my subsequently confirmed project experience. My work spanned discovery and customer walkthroughs through prototype validation and cross-functional MVP delivery.

The earlier technical documentation provides additional, narrower evidence: developed local product surfaces, role-based workflow checks, and dated acceptance records for specific areas. These are complementary to the reported school usage, not interchangeable with it.

I would not attribute an unverified percentage improvement to this project. The available source material does not establish a measured reduction in administrative time, improvement in parent engagement, paid-subscription conversion, or revenue generated. These are valuable measurements for the next phase rather than results to invent retroactively.

What I can stand behind: a structured product definition, a functional prototype used for validation, documented product decisions, a delivered MVP in school use, and a clearer framework for identifying gaps between what software supports technically and what users can actually do.

Looking Forward

With the first-school experience underway, the next step is to learn from real workflows rather than assume every feature in the wider specification deserves immediate expansion.

I would focus measurement on whether teachers consistently complete daily records, whether parents can reliably access those records, how often administrators need to resolve missing information, and what support is required for an additional school to onboard successfully. Subscription and financial workflows can then be evaluated against real operational and commercial needs.

The biggest lesson I take from KiddieDesk is that speeding up the creation of software is not the same as speeding up the creation of a usable product. AI-assisted prototyping made it easier to explore ideas and align on flows. Product management remained responsible for deciding which jobs mattered, identifying the exceptions, and ensuring that the final behavior matched the promise of the screen.

That is the work I want this case study to demonstrate: taking a complicated, multi-role business problem and turning it into something people can understand, validate, and use.
