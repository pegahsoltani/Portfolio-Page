01 · KIDDIEDESK

The challenge: one school, many connected workflows

Running a preschool involves more than managing classes and attendance. School teams handle administrative information, day-to-day care records, and communication with families. These activities involve different people, but they all rely on consistent information about children, classes, and the school day.

KiddieDesk was developed as a multi-role SaaS platform to bring these connected areas into a shared product experience. The challenge for me as a PM was not simply organizing a list of requested features. It was understanding which users needed which information, how their tasks intersected, and how to turn that complexity into an MVP that a school could actually use.

One product, different users, connected responsibilities.

02 · KIDDIEDESK

Discovery: understand the work before designing the screens

I worked with the customer to understand their needs, define the relevant user journeys, and translate the initial idea into a product scope the team could implement. The platform needed to support different contexts: school administration, teachers recording daily information, and parents following what mattered for their children. The broader product model also considered operational and platform-management responsibilities.

Rather than treating every role as a separate product, I mapped the interactions and shared information between them. The goal was to make each experience understandable while keeping the underlying workflows connected.

03 · KIDDIEDESK

Scoping the MVP: turning a complex service into usable journeys

I translated discovery into user journeys, product specifications, and acceptance criteria. The product centered on school operations, everyday care records, and parent communication, with role-appropriate views into shared information.

A useful way to explain the design is through three connected journeys:

  • School operations: organize the school context, including classes, children, and guardian relationships.
  • Daily care: support staff in recording day-to-day information and handling relevant exceptions or corrections.
  • Family communication: give parents a child-specific view of the information and updates intended for them.

These are descriptions of the product's workflow domains. They should not be presented as a definitive inventory of every MVP feature delivered unless individually verified.

04 · KIDDIEDESK

Product decisions: keeping the roles connected without overcomplicating the experience

A multi-role product forces some important questions. How much context should each user see? Which information belongs to a shared record, and which interactions are specific to a role? How do we make common school tasks straightforward without ignoring edge cases?

I used user flows, detailed requirements, and prototype walkthroughs to help the team resolve these questions before and during delivery. The product was designed around role-specific journeys grounded in a consistent school and child context, rather than making every user navigate the same interface.

I also defined a subscription-based selling model as part of the SaaS product work. The business model needed to be considered alongside product scope rather than treated solely as a later marketing decision.

05 · KIDDIEDESK

Prototype to delivery: making requirements tangible

I used ChatGPT and OpenAI Codex to help create functional prototypes from structured specifications. Walking through the proposed experience with the customer gave us something concrete to react to: we could examine end-to-end journeys, clarify ambiguous requirements, and refine the expected behavior before handing the work into full UI/UX and engineering delivery.

My role continued beyond prototyping. I coordinated with design and development, maintained delivery priorities, clarified acceptance criteria and dependencies, and followed the implementation against the agreed scope. This was cross-functional delivery, not a claim that I personally engineered the production application.

06 · KIDDIEDESK

Release and outcome: from prototype to a product in use

The most important confirmed result is straightforward: the KiddieDesk MVP was delivered and is being used by a school. Additional rollout to more schools is planned.

That matters because the project moved beyond an idea or prototype into a working product used in a real setting. I would not attach an invented efficiency percentage, user count, school count beyond one, or subscription revenue figure to that achievement.

MVP delivered · In use at one school · Further rollout planned

07 · KIDDIEDESK

Looking back: my product takeaway

This project strengthened my experience taking a customer problem across the full product lifecycle: discovery, scope definition, tangible prototype validation, and delivery with a cross-functional team.

It also reinforced a lesson about multi-role products: defining individual features is only part of the work. A PM needs to understand how people's workflows connect and make those relationships clear enough for both customers and the delivery team.

A useful MVP is not just a collection of screens. It is a set of connected journeys that people can actually work with.
